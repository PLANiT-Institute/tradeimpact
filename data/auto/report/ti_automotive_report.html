1Executive summary

Four carmakers sold 15,460,965 vehicles into four destination markets in 2024–2026. Measured against what each destination's own government has committed to, those sales are a lifetime liability of 151 MtCO₂e. Measured against what each destination is actually doing, the same sales are a net contribution of +29.0 MtCO₂e. Both numbers come from the same cars. The gap between them is the finding.

The one-sentence result. Under the observed trajectory (S1) 15 of 20 cohorts emit less over their lifetime than the fleet they join; under each government's own committed pathway (S2) none of them do — 20 out of 20, without exception, in every market and for every company.

1. Against committed policy there are no good cohorts, only slower ones

S2 turns every cohort negative: -151.5 MtCO₂e across 20 cohorts, from -19.0 to -0.41 MtCO₂e. The ranking that remains is a ranking of exposure, not of alignment.

2. Five years is the half-life of a good-news story

A vehicle's emissions are fixed on the day it is sold; the benchmark it is measured against keeps falling. Under S2 the units-weighted crossover — the year a cohort stops beating its benchmark — is 4.7 to 5.6 years after sale in every market, against 23 years in the United States under the observed trend. The committed pathway does not change the car. It changes how long the car looks good.

3. Hybrids beat the trend; only battery-electric beats the target

On the 2024 cohorts, hybrids are the largest single contribution under S1 (+13.2 MtCO₂e) and all but vanish under S2 (-21.5). Battery-electric vehicles stay positive under both (+8.0 and +4.6), and are the only powertrain that does, in 3 of the 3 markets where they appear. Combustion is the liability that dominates the total (-70.7).

4. The same car gets opposite verdicts in different markets

A Toyota Corolla HEV sold in 2024 is assessed at United States +7.7 tCO₂e per vehicle, Japan +1.3 tCO₂e per vehicle, European Union (27) -18.2 tCO₂e per vehicle, European Union (27) -19.5 tCO₂e per vehicle under committed policy. The product is the same class of technology in each; the verdict differs by 27.2 tonnes per vehicle because European Union (27) sets a benchmark that falls faster and holds a vehicle for 22 years against 13.

5. Inside Europe, where a car is registered outweighs what it is

The four companies' European cohorts, pooled and measured state by state under S1, run from -15.9 tCO₂e per vehicle in LT to +12.3 in HR — a 28 tonne swing for identical vehicles, driven by national fleet intensity, the national observed trend and national fleet age. That spread is wider than the difference between any two powertrains in the same country.

6. The answer is most sensitive to the input that is least sourced

Of the four sensitivity dimensions the model carries, vehicle lifetime moves the result the most: Japan 131 %; Korea 128 %; United States 103 %; European Union (27) 47 % of the central value. In the United States the expected lifetime is also the only tier-C input, fitted to 1977–2002 registrations. The least-evidenced number is the one the conclusion rests on, and that is stated here rather than smoothed over.

2What the number means

Trade Impact (TI) asks a counterfactual question about a single year's sales. Take the vehicles a company sold into one country in one year. Over their operating life, how much would that many vehicles have emitted had they performed like the fleet they joined — and how much will the vehicles actually sold emit? The difference is the trade impact of that sale.

TI = Σt [ Eref(t) − Eprod(t) ] × units
Eref(t) = I0 / 1000 × (1 − rfleet)t × D

I0 is the destination fleet's CO₂ intensity in the sale year (gCO₂/km), D the annual distance a vehicle of that segment travels there (km/year), rfleet the annual rate at which the benchmark improves, and t the years since sale. Battery-electric products carry the destination's grid intensity, declining at its own committed rate rpower; combustion and hybrid products carry a fixed tailpipe intensity, because a sold vehicle does not improve.

Two scenarios, and why there is no third

S1 current trajectory fits a log-linear trend to the destination's own observed series — fleet CO₂ and grid intensity, 2015 onward with 2020 and 2021 excluded as pandemic years. It answers: measured against what this country is actually doing, is this sale better or worse?

S2 committed policy is the pathway the destination's own government has stated, taken to the furthest year that government sets, so a vehicle's whole operating life sits inside the target horizon. It answers: measured against what this country has promised, is this sale consistent with it? No modelled 1.5 °C scenario appears anywhere in this report. No government published one, and the claim being made is a comparison against commitments, not against a consultant's pathway.

Reading the sign

Positive TI means the vehicles emit less over their lifetime than the benchmark fleet would have: a contribution. Negative means more: a lock-in liability, emissions the destination is committed to for as long as the vehicles stay on the road. TI is additional to Scope 3 Category 11 and never offsets it; it is a measure of what a trade flow commits a market to, not a credit against a company's own inventory.

3Scope, and what it covers

The unit of analysis is a cohort: one company, one destination market, one sale year. There are 20 of them here. Every cohort is built from the company's or the market's own published volumes, joined to certified product values from that market's own regulator, and measured against a benchmark built from that market's own statistics. Cohorts and markets are never summed: they rest on different sales bases, test cycles and national benchmarks.

5Against committed policy, there are no good cohorts

Finding 1

Under each destination's own committed pathway, not one of the 20 cohorts emits less over its lifetime than the fleet it joins.

The size of the liability tracks three things, in order: how far the destination's committed pathway falls over a vehicle's life, how long that life is, and how far the vehicles travel each year. It tracks the product mix fourth. That ordering is the uncomfortable part of the result: the same product mix sold into a market with a steeper commitment and a longer-lived fleet produces a larger liability, and no product decision inside the company changes the slope of a national target.

The five cohorts already negative under the observed trend

Under S1, 5 of 20 cohorts are already liabilities, all of them in European Union (27) and United States: Toyota 2024 -1.68 Mt; Hyundai 2024 -1.66 Mt; Kia 2024 -1.52 Mt; Nissan 2024 -1.44 Mt; Kia 2026 -0.49 Mt. Europe is the only market where the observed trend alone is enough to make a 2024 sale a liability, for two compounding reasons: its observed per-car CO₂ intensity is falling faster than any other market's, and its fleets are the oldest, so a car sold in 2024 is measured against an improving benchmark for up to two decades.

What would change this. Not a marginally better hybrid. A cohort turns positive under S2 only if its product intensity starts far enough below the benchmark that the benchmark's decline cannot catch it within the vehicle's life — which in practice means a zero-tailpipe product in a market whose grid is also decarbonising, or a much shorter assumed vehicle life. Section 9 shows the arithmetic on a single nameplate.

6Five years is the half-life of a good-news story

Finding 2

Under committed policy a cohort stops beating its benchmark 4.7 to 5.6 years after it is sold — in every market, whatever the product mix.

The crossover is not an artefact of the model; it is the arithmetic of selling a fixed asset into a moving target. A vehicle's tailpipe intensity is set at manufacture. The benchmark is a national fleet average that a government has committed to lowering every year. Whatever margin a product starts with is spent at the benchmark's rate of decline, and under committed policy that rate is 6.6 to 13.5 per cent a year.

Where the trend gives cover, and where it does not

Under S1 the crossovers are far apart: 23 years in the United States, 18 in Japan, 11 in the EU27, and in Korea no crossover at all for any of the 1,364,996 units assessed. Korea's observed road-transport CO₂ is essentially flat, so a Korean cohort never has to outrun anything. That is the clearest illustration of what S1 measures: not the product's quality, but the destination's inertia.

Why the crossover matters more than the total. A lifetime total nets a few good early years against many bad later ones and reports one number. The crossover year says when the policy problem starts. For a fleet operator or a regulator, a cohort that turns at year five is a different object from one that turns at year twenty, even where the lifetime totals match.

7The flat line and the falling line

Finding 3

One cohort, year by year: the product line is flat by construction, the benchmark line falls by commitment, and everything follows from the two crossing.

The product line falls by 0.2 per cent over 13 years — only because the battery-electric part of the cohort charges from a grid that decarbonises. The combustion and hybrid parts do not move at all. The S2 benchmark falls 56 per cent over the same window. That asymmetry is the whole mechanism.

Read the two figures together. Cumulative trade impact peaks at +1.31 MtCO₂e in 2025, and by 2036 it has fallen to -19.00. A report that stopped at the peak would call this cohort a contribution. A report that stops at the end of the vehicles' life — which is what the whitepaper requires, and what a lock-in claim means — calls it a liability. Both numbers are in ti_annual.csv; the choice of horizon is the difference between them.

8Hybrids beat the trend, not the target

Finding 4

Hybrids are the largest contribution against what markets are doing, and almost nothing against what they have promised. Battery-electric is the only powertrain that stays positive under both.

The hybrid result is the one worth pausing on. Across the 2024 cohorts, hybrids carry +13.2 MtCO₂e against the observed trend and -21.5 against committed policy. A technology that looks like the main decarbonisation lever in the first framing disappears entirely in the second. Nothing about the vehicles changed between the two columns; only the yardstick did.

The mechanism is the fixed intensity again. A hybrid's advantage over the fleet a market currently runs is real, and over a lifetime it is worth 13.5 tonnes a vehicle. Measured against the pathway the same markets have committed to, those vehicles are a 22.0 tonne liability each instead: the committed pathway spends the margin in a handful of years, and the hybrid then sits above the benchmark for the rest of its life. Battery-electric products escape this because their emissions fall with the grid: they are the only product in the cohort whose intensity is not fixed at the point of sale.

The policy reading. If a market's question is "are these sales better than our current fleet?", hybrids answer yes. If the question is "are these sales consistent with the pathway we have committed to?", only zero-tailpipe products answer yes, and only where the grid is on its own committed pathway too. Those are different questions and they have different answers for the same cars.

Four market parameters do all the work in that table, and none of them is a property of the car.

  • The benchmark level. A destination's fleet intensity sets the starting margin. The United States benchmark is the all-light-duty fleet including pickups, so a compact hybrid starts far below it; Japan's fleet average already contains the hybrids and kei cars that make a Japanese cohort look clean elsewhere, so the same hybrid starts barely below it.
  • Annual distance. Every kilogram per kilometre is multiplied by it. US light-duty vehicles travel roughly twice as far each year as Japanese cars, so both the margin and the later liability are about twice the size.
  • Vehicle life. Europe holds a car for close to two decades and Japan and the US for about thirteen years, which decides how many years of a falling benchmark the vehicle has to survive.
  • The rate of decline. The steepest committed pathway in this report is more than twice the shallowest. A product that would be defensible against one government's commitment is not against another's.

What this means for a company's strategy. Product planning cannot be separated from destination planning. The same model line, shifted between two markets in the mix, changes the assessed lifetime liability by more than a full powertrain generation of efficiency improvement would. It also means a company cannot be ranked on trade impact without stating where it sells, which is why every table in this report carries a market column and no table sums across markets.

Luxembourg is excluded entirely: its registration-based fleet intensity is implausible for a country whose fleet is largely cross-border.

11Why the markets are never summed

A trade impact figure is only as meaningful as the benchmark behind it, and the benchmarks in this report are built from four different countries' statistics. They are not interchangeable.

Across the 27 EU member states assessed, fleet intensity runs from 98 to 389 gCO₂/km and the operating life from 12 to 25 years, so the EU27 rows are a distribution rather than a single benchmark. The single-country markets each have one.

Three asymmetries worth naming

  • The United States benchmark is generous by construction. Its emissions numerator is all light-duty vehicles including pickups and large SUVs, because the distance and stock statistics split the fleet by wheelbase rather than by body type and pairing a car numerator with a short-wheelbase denominator produces an implausible figure. A benchmark that includes pickups is higher than a car-only benchmark, and a higher benchmark makes every product look better. The segment ratio stays at 1.0 and the consequence is disclosed rather than corrected.
  • Japan's benchmark is unusually hard to beat. Its fleet average already contains kei cars and a decade of hybrid sales, so it is the lowest passenger-car intensity in the report while the cohort measured against it excludes kei cars entirely.
  • Korea's numerator is a proxy. The national inventory publishes no vehicle-class split, so the class share comes from a bottom-up local inventory whose national level sits 13–26 per cent below the inventory total. Only the share is used, never the level, and every Korean fleet-intensity value is tier C because of it.

This is why no table in this report adds a company's markets together. A single global figure would be dominated by whichever market happened to have the most generous benchmark and the longest assumed vehicle life, and would move if a company merely shifted its sales mix between two countries.

Korea is where this matters most, because Hyundai's Porter and Kia's Bongo one-tonne trucks are a large share of Korean domestic volume. Measured against Korean goods vehicles — 428 gCO₂/km over 17,363 km a year for 13 years, against 203 gCO₂/km over 11,963 km for 11 years for cars — the freight cohort is the single largest per-vehicle contribution in the whole report under S1 (28.5 tCO₂e per vehicle) and a substantial liability under S2 (-15.3).

Japan carries a goods segment on the same principle; buses are not built there because the national distance survey bundles petrol buses in with cars and special vehicles, which would give an all-bus emissions numerator a diesel-only denominator.

Four biases that run the other way

Not every gap is neutral, and three of the four known biases in this report flatter the companies:

  • The US benchmark includes pickups and large SUVs, which raises it and makes every US product look better than a car-only comparison would.
  • Japan's real-world correction factor is borrowed from the European on-board fuel-consumption programme for the same test procedure; Japan's own cycle omits the highest-speed phase, so the true gap is probably larger and the factor understates real-world product emissions.
  • Korea's committed pathway is applied to intensity rather than to absolute emissions while its fleet is still growing, which makes the intensity-only reading looser than the absolute target.
  • Against that, withholding battery-electric units in Japan removes the cleanest products from the Japanese cohorts and makes those results worse than complete data would.

Every one of these is on the affected rows in the output tables, not only in this paragraph.

What makes each market tier C is different, and worth knowing before quoting a figure:

  • United States — the expected vehicle lifetime. It is the NHTSA survival schedule, fitted to 1977–2002 registrations rather than to the current fleet. Every other US input is tier A or B.
  • Korea — the fleet intensity, because the national inventory publishes no vehicle-class split, and the lifetime, derived from a mean fleet age whose oldest band is open-ended.
  • Japan — the powertrain split and the borrowed real-world factor. Its emissions numerator, uniquely, is tier A: Japan publishes road CO₂ by vehicle type.
  • EU27 — the distance for the thirteen states with no national figure, and the lifetime everywhere, derived from mean fleet age.

How to quote these results. The direction and the ordering are robust: they survive every sensitivity variant the model carries. The magnitudes are not, and should be quoted with the band in section 15, not as point estimates.

15What would change the answer

Four inputs are varied one at a time over their published or documented ranges, and every cohort is repriced. The bars below are the resulting spread around the central figure for the 2024 cohorts under committed policy, largest spread first.

Vehicle lifetime dominates everywhere: Japan 131 %; Korea 128 %; United States 103 %; European Union (27) 47 % of the central value, against under 22 % for the next largest. That is a structural feature of the method rather than a defect in one market's data: the lifetime is both the number of years of benchmark decline a vehicle has to survive and the multiplier on every year's gap, so it enters the result twice.

The powertrain-mix bound matters where a sales release does not split a nameplate by powertrain and the split has to be assumed from a market-wide share. Where such a bound exists, pricing every unit of the affected nameplates as a hybrid moves the cohort result by 82 % at most — large enough that the assumption is named in the method document and carried as a variant rather than buried.

What no sensitivity variant does. None of them flips a committed-policy result from liability to contribution. Taking three years off every vehicle's life — the most favourable single variant in the model — reduces the liabilities but does not remove one of them. The finding in section 5 is robust to every parameter the model is willing to vary.

16How to reproduce this

Everything in this report is regenerated from the raw sources by one command, and every intermediate table is content-hashed so a figure can be traced to the exact bytes it came from.

.venv/bin/python script/auto/run_all.py
.venv/bin/python script/auto/report/build_report.py

The first command fetches or reuses every raw file, re-derives every processed dataset, rebuilds the model, writes the SQLite database, and runs the linter and the test suite, exiting non-zero at the first failure. The second reads that database and writes this file. No number in this report is typed by hand; the prose interpolates the same query results the charts use, so a sentence cannot drift away from its table.
